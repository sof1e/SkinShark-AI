# Moles (Nevi)
![Moles](assets/images/mole_1.jpg)

Moles, or nevi, are common skin growths caused by clusters of pigment cells (melanocytes). Most people develop 10–45 moles during childhood and adolescence. Moles may change in appearance or fade over time.

Most moles are harmless, but rarely, they can become cancerous. Monitoring your moles for changes is important for early detection of skin cancer, especially melanoma.

---

## Symptoms

- **Color & Texture:** Moles can be brown, tan, black, blue, red, or pink. They may be smooth, wrinkled, flat, or raised, and sometimes have hair.
- **Shape:** Usually oval or round.
- **Size:** Typically less than 6 mm (¼ inch) in diameter, about the size of a pencil eraser. Congenital nevi (present at birth) can be larger.

Moles can appear anywhere on the body, including the scalp, armpits, under nails, and between fingers or toes. Hormonal changes (teenage years, pregnancy) may cause moles to darken or enlarge.

**Note:** Clusters of brown spots (dermatoses papulosa nigra) are noncancerous and more common in Black women. These are not moles and do not increase melanoma risk.

---

## Types of Moles

- **Common acquired nevi:** Small, round, and evenly colored. Most people have these.
- **Congenital nevi:** Present at birth, can be larger and have a higher risk of becoming cancerous.
- **Atypical (dysplastic) nevi:** Larger than common moles, with irregular borders and color variation. These may run in families and carry a higher melanoma risk.

## Warning Signs for Melanoma

Watch for the ABCDEs:

- **A**symmetry: One half is unlike the other.
- **B**order: Irregular, notched, or scalloped edges.
- **C**olor: Multiple or uneven colors.
- **D**iameter: Larger than 6 mm or growing.
- **E**volving: Changes in size, shape, color, or symptoms (itching, bleeding).

Not all cancerous moles show all these signs.

## When to See a Doctor

See a healthcare professional if a mole looks unusual, grows, or changes in any way.

---

## Causes

Moles form when melanocytes cluster together in the skin. Melanocytes produce melanin, the pigment that gives skin its color.

---

## Complications

The main risk is melanoma (skin cancer). Risk factors include:

- Large congenital nevi (present at birth, >20 cm).
- Atypical/dysplastic nevi (large, irregular moles, often familial).
- Having more than 50 moles.
- Personal or family history of melanoma.
- Use of tanning beds or lamps.

## Diagnosis

A healthcare provider will examine your skin and may use a dermatoscope (a special magnifying tool) to look at moles more closely. If a mole appears suspicious, a biopsy may be performed to check for cancer cells.

---

## Treatment

Most moles do not require treatment. If a mole is bothersome, unattractive, or suspicious for cancer, it can be removed by:

- **Surgical excision:** Cutting out the mole and stitching the skin.
- **Shave removal:** Shaving off the mole at skin level.
- **Laser removal:** Sometimes used for non-cancerous moles.

Never try to remove a mole at home.

---

## Prevention

- **Monitor your skin:** Regularly check your moles for changes. Do monthly self-exams, including hard-to-see areas (scalp, nails, armpits, between toes, genital area).
- **Protect your skin:** 
    - Avoid sun exposure during peak hours (10 a.m.–2 p.m.).
    - Use broad-spectrum sunscreen (SPF 30+), reapplying every 2 hours and after swimming or sweating.
    - Wear protective clothing (hats, sunglasses, long sleeves).
    - Avoid tanning beds and lamps.

Discuss your risk factors and need for professional skin exams with your healthcare provider.

---

## Key Points

- Most moles are harmless, but monitor for changes.
- Early detection of melanoma improves outcomes.
- Protect your skin from UV exposure to reduce risk.

## When to See a Doctor

Consult a healthcare provider if you notice persistent skin changes, discomfort, or if symptoms worsen.
